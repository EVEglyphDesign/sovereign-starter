# The case for the harness

**A briefing for partners, programme directors, general counsel and finance.**

`EgD-SS-CASE-001` · Companion to [`README.md`](./README.md) and
[`SOVEREIGN-STARTER.md`](./SOVEREIGN-STARTER.md) · Reviewed 19 September 2026

---

## Summary

A consulting practice that uses AI on client work now has three exposures it did
not have two years ago:

1. **A regulatory exposure.** Several regimes now require an organisation to
   *demonstrate* how an AI system was governed and used. Demonstration is a
   documentary act. You cannot demonstrate what you did not record.
2. **An economic exposure.** AI is a metered input with no natural unit-cost
   discipline. Most practices cannot state what a unit of AI-assisted work costs
   them, which means they cannot price it, forecast it, or reduce it.
3. **A capital exposure.** The working knowledge a practice accumulates is
   increasingly its principal asset, and it is increasingly held in a form that
   cannot be valued, transferred, audited or defended.

The harness — a first-read instruction file plus an append-only observation
register, connected by an escalation rule — is a single, low-cost control that
addresses all three. It is not a framework, a methodology or a product. It is a
record-keeping discipline that happens to be the cheapest available answer to
three otherwise expensive problems.

This document sets out that argument. It is not legal or accounting advice;
the obligations that apply to any particular firm depend on its jurisdiction,
role and client contracts, and should be confirmed with counsel and auditors.

## Part 1 — The governing legislation around usage

The direction of travel across every major regime is the same: **the obligation
is not to use AI well, it is to be able to show that you used AI well.** The
burden of proof sits with the deployer.

### The EU AI Act — now live, not forthcoming

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 entered into force on **1 August 2024**. Its
obligations have landed in stages:

| Date | What applied |
|---|---|
| 2 February 2025 | Prohibited AI practices; **AI literacy obligations** |
| 2 August 2025 | Governance rules; obligations for general-purpose AI models |
| **2 August 2026** | **General application, with some exceptions** — including the transparency requirements |
| 2 December 2027 | Systems used in certain high-risk areas |
| 2 August 2028 | High-risk systems integrated into products |

Two of these matter disproportionately to a consulting practice.

**The AI literacy obligation has applied since February 2025.** It requires
providers and deployers to take measures to ensure a sufficient level of AI
literacy among staff dealing with AI systems on their behalf. A firm asked to
evidence this has two options: assert that its people are competent, or produce
a record. A first-read instruction file that every practitioner has adapted in
their own words, paired with a register of the misses they observed and the
rules they wrote in response, *is* that record. It is contemporaneous, it is
attributable, and it demonstrates an active practice rather than a policy PDF
nobody opened.

**General application arrived on 2 August 2026** — six weeks before this
document was written. Firms still treating the AI Act as a 2027 problem are
already inside the regime.

### GDPR accountability

Article 5(2) of the GDPR has required controllers to be *responsible for, and
able to demonstrate compliance with*, the data-processing principles since 2018.
Where AI is used on personal data — which in an SAP context means most finance,
HR and customer work — that demonstrative burden extends to the AI step. A
register recording what the AI was instructed to do, what it did instead, and
what rule was written in response, speaks directly to that obligation in a way
that a tool licence does not.

### The EU Data Act

Regulation (EU) 2023/2854 has applied since **12 September 2025**. It governs
access to and sharing of data generated by connected products and related
services, and it constrains what a provider may do with data it holds on a
customer's behalf. Its practical effect on consulting work is upstream of any
AI question: it forces an explicit answer to *who controls this data, under what
terms, and what may be done with it* — an answer that has to be recorded
somewhere durable, per engagement.

### ISO/IEC 42001

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 is the AI management system standard — the certifiable
counterpart to ISO 27001 for information security. Its structure is the
familiar management-system loop: state your policy, operate it, record what
happened, review, improve. A firm pursuing certification will be asked for
evidence of exactly that loop. The harness is a minimal, honest implementation
of it that can be running before any certification programme starts, rather
than assembled retrospectively to satisfy an auditor.

### What this part amounts to

Four regimes, one shared demand: **a contemporaneous, attributable record of how
the AI was governed and what it actually did.** Firms will meet this either by
buying a governance platform, or by keeping a disciplined text record from the
first day of use. The second is available now, costs nothing, and produces the
evidence in the order events actually occurred — which is the only order an
auditor finds credible.

## Part 2 — The economics

### AI is a metered input priced like a fixed cost

Most practices bought AI as seats. Seats look like a fixed cost, so nobody
measures consumption per unit of output. The result is that a firm can tell you
what AI costs per head per month, and cannot tell you what it costs to produce
one deliverable — which is the only figure that lets you price the work.

The observation register fixes this from the opposite end. It does not measure
tokens; it measures **rework**. Every row records an ask, what was produced
instead, the cheaper path that existed, and the cost borne. Rows are individually
imprecise. In aggregate they are the first defensible statement a practice can
make about where its AI spend is actually going.

### Rework is the real cost, and it is invisible by default

The expensive failure mode is not the AI refusing to help. It is the AI
producing something plausible that has to be redone — and the redoing being
absorbed silently by whoever noticed. That absorption is why the cost never
reaches a management report. A register makes it reach one, because logging the
row is a thirty-second act and the aggregate is unarguable.

### The rule of three is a cost control, not a ritual

Three observations of the same shape trigger a rule change. This is the
mechanism that converts evidence into reduced unit cost:

- **Below three,** you have anecdotes, and rewriting your instructions on an
  anecdote is how instruction files bloat into unusable documents.
- **At three,** you have a pattern, and a rule written against it closes a
  recurring cost rather than a one-off annoyance.
- **After the rule,** the register tells you whether it worked. If the shape
  stops recurring, the rule was correct. If it keeps recurring, the rule was
  under-specified and the evidence says so.

That feedback loop is ordinary continuous improvement applied to a cost line
most firms are not yet measuring. Nothing about it is novel. What is novel is
that the cost line exists at all.

### The commercial argument to a client

A practice that can show a client its AI usage register is making a claim no
competitor making assertions can match: *we measure our own rework, here is the
trend, and here is the rule we wrote when the trend told us to.* In a market
where every firm claims AI-enabled delivery, evidence of self-measurement is the
differentiator that survives procurement scrutiny.

## Part 3 — Data as a capital asset

This is the argument that should lead any conversation with a CFO.

### The national accounts changed

The **2025 System of National Accounts** — the international statistical standard
that defines how economies measure output and capital — recognises **data as a
produced asset**. Eurostat has published a companion *Handbook on measuring data
in the System of National Accounts* (2025 edition) setting out how to do it.
Statistically, data is now capital formation, not consumption. The question
"is our data an asset?" has been answered at the level of the standard.

### Corporate accounting has not caught up

IAS 38 continues to prohibit recognition of most internally generated intangibles
on the balance sheet. Internally generated goodwill, brands and similar items are
expressly excluded, and the recognition criteria for internally generated
intangibles are demanding. The practical consequence is well documented: the gap
between the market value and the book value of knowledge-intensive firms is
largely made up of assets the accounts are not permitted to show.

So the position as of 2026 is:

> **Statistically, data is capital. On the balance sheet, it is mostly expense.
> The asset is real, it is growing, and it is invisible.**

### What turns an expense into a capital asset

Whatever the accounting treatment, the *economic* characteristics that make data
behave like a capital asset are not mysterious. It must be:

- **Durable** — it survives the engagement, the tool, the employee and the
  vendor relationship.
- **Attributable** — its origin is recorded, so a figure can be defended six
  months later in front of someone hostile.
- **Controlled** — the firm can grant, withhold and revoke access to it.
- **Transferable** — it can move to a successor without a rebuild.

Now compare that list against where consulting knowledge actually lives:
a chat history inside a vendor's product, a shared drive of undated documents,
a partner's laptop, someone's memory. None of those four properties holds. The
asset is being consumed at the moment it is created.

### The harness produces the missing properties

A repository carrying a first-read instruction file, an append-only observation
register, and provenance on every input, is the minimum structure that satisfies
all four:

| Property | How the harness delivers it |
|---|---|
| Durable | Plain text under version control. Outlives any AI vendor, licence or seat. |
| Attributable | Every input records its source; every change carries an author and a date. |
| Controlled | Repository access is granted, audited and revoked as an explicit act. |
| Transferable | The whole record moves as a clone. Nothing depends on one person's memory. |

That is the argument in one line: **the harness is the cheapest mechanism that
converts AI-assisted work from a consumed expense into a retained, auditable,
transferable asset.**

### The convergence

Notice that Parts 1, 2 and 3 demand the same artefact. The regulator wants a
contemporaneous record of AI governance and use. The finance director wants a
measured unit cost and a reducible rework line. The CFO — and eventually the
auditor — wants durable, attributable, controlled, transferable data.

All three are satisfied by the same discipline: **write down what you told the
AI to do, write down what it actually did, and keep both somewhere with a
history.** The firms that start recording now will hold years of defensible
evidence when it is asked for. The firms that start when it is asked for will
be reconstructing it from memory, which is both expensive and unconvincing.

## What to do in the first week

1. **Adopt the file.** One practitioner, one live engagement, the unedited
   starter. Do not run a programme.
2. **Write Part I in your own words.** Delete every worked rule that does not
   describe your practice. What remains is your actual standard, which is worth
   more than a comprehensive one nobody follows.
3. **Log rows for two weeks without analysing them.** Resist drawing conclusions
   early; the value is in the aggregate, and a fortnight is enough to get one.
4. **Apply the rule of three once.** Find the first shape that recurred three
   times, write the rule, and watch whether it closes.
5. **Then decide whether to extend it.** A method that survived one engagement
   in one practitioner's hands is worth taking to a partner meeting. One adopted
   by mandate before it was tested is not.
